# Executing Work

The contract: once the spec is approved, proceed autonomously to completion. No per-step check-ins, no "should you continue?", no gating individual edits. The spec is the agreement; execute it.

Interrupt me only for: a contradiction inside the spec, a decision the spec does not cover with material consequences, a destructive or irreversible action, or a systematic-debugging dead end (per that skill's stop-and-report rule). Everything else is yours to resolve and record.

## The completion contract

The spec is the goal. Once execution starts, run every remaining unblocked section to completion in this session. A section boundary is not a stopping point. A long-running gate is not a stopping point. Context pressure is not a stopping point. The only reason to stop mid-spec is a true blocker, and when you hit one you make it impossible to miss.

For an externally-driven worker (the External-engine stand-down), the directed section is the whole goal: finishing that one section and stopping is completion under this contract, never an early stop, because the engine verifies the section and spawns the next worker itself.

This is the rule that fails most often under the pressure of a long run, so it is stated as a hard prohibition, not a preference.

**Do not end your turn** to:

- report progress between sections ("§3 done, say the word and start §4"). Close the section and start the next.
- wait on a build, test suite, or Live gate ("holding for the gate, ~2 min"). Wait on it in-turn: background it and poll a readiness signal (`until` on a marker or exit code), then continue when it returns.
- manage context ("pausing here rather than open §7 at the tail of a long run"). The Chapter plus the SessionStart resume hook make a fresh session lossless; starting one is my call, never your reason to halt.
- await a dispatched subagent ("holding while the implementer builds §3.1b"). A background agent (`run_in_background: true`, the Agent-tool default) ends your turn to await its completion notification, and under an armed leash that turn-end is a stop the hook blocks and re-bills your whole context on, the `WAITING:` shape below being the one turn-end the hook does allow and never the shape this row is about. A wait is not a stop here either: keep the turn alive. Dispatch a single critical-path implementer synchronously (`run_in_background: false`) so its whole run is one in-turn call, unless the dispatch carries a model override, which is refused the synchronous shape whatever its length and waits on the in-turn `TaskOutput` loop below instead, since the synchronous call is the one place finishing-work's first-turn reading cannot be taken and an override dispatch is the shape that reading exists to catch; for any other live agent (a parallel fan-out member, or an agent continued via SendMessage, which always resumes in the background), wait in-turn on real task status: `TaskOutput(task_id, block: true)` blocks until the run completes, resumed runs included, but returns at its own `timeout` first, which is thirty seconds where the parameter is omitted and ten minutes at the highest value it takes, so loop the call until status reads completed, capping each call's `timeout` per the `WAITING:` stop shape's hold mechanism below while finishing-work's unavailability rule has any window pending on an in-flight dispatch, so no window elapses whole inside a call. A call that times out mid-run returns a raw truncated slice of the agent's JSONL transcript inline (thinking blocks and tool payloads, not the report); only a call whose status reads completed yields the final message, so treat a timeout's payload as the expected cost of an overrun and call again, never as the result and never, on its own, as a stall; only the wedge hallmark, which finishing-work's unavailability rule owns, makes a stall out of silence, and that rule owns the cadence on which this loop evaluates it. Never infer completion from transcript-file quiescence either: a mid-run pause reads as done, and completion is read from task status or nowhere. Where the wedge hallmark calls for a growth reading, take it the way finishing-work's rule specifies, at the artifact it names. Never end the turn on a completion notification while a leash is armed, and never clear the leash to escape the block: that abandons the very continuity it exists to hold.

Rationalization table (the excuse, and why it is wrong):

| The excuse | Why it is wrong |
|---|---|
| "This is a clean boundary to pause at." | Clean boundaries are for resuming, not for stopping with work left. Continue. |
| "Holding for the gate." | A wait is not a stop. Poll the gate in-turn and continue. |
| "It is the tail of a long run, safer to stop." | The Chapter plus resume hook protect you. Context is my call, not a stop condition. |
| "Let me confirm before continuing." | The approved spec is the confirmation. Continue unless a true blocker hits. |
| "I'll end the turn to await the dispatched agent's notification." | Awaiting is a wait, and a wait is not a stop. Foreground the dispatch (`run_in_background: false`; a dispatch carrying a model override is refused that shape and takes the `TaskOutput` block instead) or block on `TaskOutput` in-turn; do not end the turn, and do not clear the leash to get out of the block. |
| "I don't have enough context left to review the next round properly." | Compaction gives you a fresh window and the plan doc carries the state. The choice is not stop-versus-ship-unreviewed; it is compact-and-review versus abandon. Continue. |

The table is instances, not the boundary: any reason to stop with unblocked work remaining that is not in the blocker set below is wrong the same way, whether or not a row names it.

Red flags that you are about to stop wrongly: "say the word and continue", "holding for", "paused here", "at the tail of", "ready to continue when you are", "holding while the agent builds", "awaiting the notification". If you are about to write one of these with unblocked work remaining, do not. Keep going.

**Stop only for a true blocker, and make it loud.** The blocker set:

- an external dependency only I can satisfy (a GUI action like a Docker memory bump, a cloud resource that must be provisioned, a credential or secret you cannot reach),
- a contradiction inside the spec, or a material decision the spec does not cover,
- a destructive or irreversible action that needs my yes,
- a systematic-debugging dead end.

The set is closed. Capacity is never on it: native compaction gives a fresh window with the leash intact, and the plan doc plus its Chapters carry the state, so a stop reasoned from context is a stop dressed as a blocker. The `WAITING:` shape's two occasions below, a turn whose only remaining work is dispatched background subagents and a park at a safe boundary taken on a request per the park skill, are not on the set and do not need to be: each ends a turn with the leash armed rather than work stranded, the dispatch occasion pending the completion notification that re-invokes the session, the park with the record written and the resume named by the park skill's own steps, so neither is the stop with unblocked work stranded that this set governs, and neither is a route to one, a park being taken on a request rather than on the run's own judgment of its own work.

**Before any BLOCKED at all, the expert ask goes out, and it goes ahead of the consult.** Where the repo has a live expert seat on the roster, the blocker goes to it before you declare; the peer-sessions skill owns that route, the seats it runs between, and the bounds on both ends, and this paragraph is where the moment lives. It goes first because it is the cheaper instrument and the one that resolves from sources that already exist, and it never gates: send it, keep working whatever is still workable, and declare at exactly the point you would have with no ask in flight. What an answer discharges is narrower than it looks, and the two instruments are asking different questions. The ask is about whether the answer already exists somewhere, so it prevents a declaration only where it hands you something you verify on your own surface, an existing source that answers the question with its provenance traced, or a diagnosis you can reproduce; a peer's say-so is a claim to check rather than the thing that settles it. The consult is about whether your framing is wrong, so it still runs on whatever decision survives the ask, and the ask never stands in for it. Neither discharges a blocker that exists because only I may say yes. Where no expert seat is live there is no ask at all, and you go straight to the consult path below. On declaring, also notify this machine's live coordinator seat where one is up, which routes the escalation rather than resolving it, and the expert too where the ask went unanswered. The ask and the notice carry the same public-board cap the first-line paragraph below puts on the declaration's first line, and they carry it as a standard rather than as a reading of where either lands, stated against a public board so that moving the board somewhere quieter never reads as relaxing it. Reach is what puts each leg in scope, never what warrants the cap on it: the ask goes to an address nothing corroborates, any session being able to answer to an expert's name, and the notice is what the coordinator briefs from. `docs/security-model.md` carries the readership analysis behind the standard, and the coordinator skill owns the precondition it names, which bounds what that seat may land rather than what a sender may send. And an ask that prevents a declaration is an agreement reached over messaging, so the record rule binds it exactly as it binds any other: the resolved ask lands in the plan doc in the same turn, or a post-compaction session re-hits the blocker with nothing to show it was answered.

**Before any BLOCKED that turns on a decision, the consult runs too, after the ask and before the declaration.** Two shapes in the set above are not decisions to rule on and go straight up: an external dependency only I can satisfy, and a destructive action waiting on my yes. For the rest, a spec gap is mine to answer only where the answer turns on preference, cost, or risk appetite; where it turns on facts about the system it is rulable, and a mixed question is ruled first so that what reaches me is the small real fork. The BLOCKED that survives carries the ruling in its decision brief; the consult skill owns the mechanics.

When you stop, the message's very first characters are `BLOCKED: <exactly what you need from me>`, so I see it in seconds rather than discovering a silent halt hours later. The body under the prefix is a decision brief in the doctrine's client-briefing register (situation, decision, stakes, options with trade-offs, argued recommendation, what happens while it goes unanswered, evidence references at the end): a BLOCKED lands on my phone hours later with no session context, so it must be decidable from the brief alone. The bare prefix opens the message: no close-out summary above it, no bold or heading wrapping it; put any shipped-work recap after the BLOCKED paragraph. The Stop hook releases only on that exact leading prefix and deliberately ignores a `BLOCKED:` sitting mid-message (quoting the convention must never release the leash), so a summary-first stop bounces and costs an extra turn. A release whose BLOCKED line gives capacity as its reason (context, compaction, a fresh session) is refused the same way: capacity is not in the set, so that stop bounces too. A progress update is not a stop and must not be written as one. The `/goal` Stop hook is a backstop, not the mechanism; this contract is the mechanism.

**The first line carries only what you would put on a public board, because it travels further than the rest of the message.** The rest of the declaration is addressed to me alone. The first line is not: on a mid-queue advance the Stop hook records it into the blocked plan's outcome note, stripped to printable ASCII and cut at 120 characters with no truncation mark, and this machine's coordinator seat reads that note onto an operator brief and onto that seat's board in the memory store. So compose that one line for a public board and keep it inside 120 characters, since what runs past is dropped mid-clause and reads downstream as though it were whole. The cap is a standard rather than a reading of where the board sits, stated against a public board so that moving the board somewhere quieter never reads as relaxing it; `docs/security-model.md` carries the readership analysis behind it, and the coordinator skill owns the precondition it names, which bounds what that seat may land rather than what a sender may send. Spell any path in it repo-relative rather than absolute, the reason being worth knowing rather than just obeying: an absolute worktree path embeds the OS username on the default layout. The cap holds whatever the queue position, and the recording is what varies: the last plan of a queue records no note at all, while the coordinator notice carries that same line either way. The peer-sessions skill states the same cap over the messages this route adds, and it is restated here because this is the paragraph a leashed run actually reads while a worker mid-run may never load that one.

Waiting is the third stop shape, and it has two occasions. One is a turn whose only remaining work is background subagents already dispatched: do not hold the turn open with a foreground wait, and do not dress the pause as a blocker; end the turn with `WAITING:` as the very first characters, naming the dispatch that is pending. The other is a park at a safe boundary per the park skill, taken on a request rather than on the run's own judgment of its own work, the operator-declared update window being the occasion that ordinarily produces one; the request is the predicate because it is the one the receiving session can evaluate, the window's operator-initiated condition sitting with the coordinator that declares it, the party that can establish it, and never with the receiver: there the park skill's drain runs first, every live dispatch finished or explicitly stopped by its steps, and only once nothing is left in flight does the turn end on the same `WAITING:` lead, naming the park and its ground as the park skill's ground rule states it, which for a park taken on a relayed request is the request itself rather than the window that request pointed at. A `WAITING:` on a wedged dispatch sleeps the run with nothing left to wake it, since a wedge never completes and so never re-invokes the session. Where an in-flight dispatch's growth window or its first-turn window has already closed by parking time (finishing-work's unavailability rule defines both readings and owns their windows), run the hallmark check through to its probe, or, where the pair reads synthetic-only, through the TaskStop that route begins with, before writing this line: the first-turn window is readable from the dispatch record you hold at the moment you park, and the growth window is too in its elapsed-since-dispatch fallback form, its own opening, the last observed growth, being a session observation the record does not carry. Where a dispatch is young enough that its first-turn reading is still pending, do not park yet, which is a hold rather than a refusal: that window closes minutes after dispatch, at the point finishing-work's rule sets, so a park written inside it leaves the window to close with nobody looking, and a `WAITING:` stop on the dispatch occasion written between that close and the growth window's, where the reading came back both counts zero, sleeps the run on a dispatch that may never have started. Hold the turn in-turn per the completion contract until every in-flight dispatch's first-turn reading has been taken and resolved on the pair it returns: a pair reading both counts zero is run through its probe, while a synthetic-only pair, a zero non-synthetic count beside a non-zero `<synthetic>` one, is granted no probe by the owning rule and takes its TaskStop at the first-turn window's close instead. The re-dispatch half of that route belongs to the dispatch occasion alone, because a park may leave behind a finished agent or a stopped one and never a fresh dispatch: a turn ending on the dispatch occasion runs the route whole and waits on the successor's own reading, while a parking session stops the agent, records in the interim board entry what it was asked and that it never started, and leaves the re-dispatch to the session that resumes; park only on what survives. The hold carries its mechanism, because the only in-turn hold the contract names is a blocking `TaskOutput`, which returns at its own `timeout` rather than at the run's end, and a window running inside that call is observed only at that return, which at the highest value the parameter takes can land as much as ten minutes past the window: while any of the unavailability rule's windows is pending on an in-flight dispatch, a first-turn reading, a growth window, or a running probe window, cap the block by passing `TaskOutput`'s `timeout` parameter, which on v2.1.205 is a millisecond count taking 30000 where it is omitted and 600000 as its maximum, so the ten-minute figure is the ceiling on what you may pass and never what a bare call gives you: set it to the lesser of the time remaining until the earliest pending window closes and that 600000 maximum, rather than to a window's full length, because the window runs from its own opening while the call runs from its own start, so a cap set to the full length lands the return past the close by exactly that gap, while a remainder longer than the maximum is no value the call takes at all. The return then lands at the moment the reading becomes meaningful, or, where that remainder ran past the maximum, early enough to look, re-block, and land there on a later call. The cap is not this pre-park hold's alone: it rides every blocking `TaskOutput` the completion contract loops while such a window is pending, since the awaiting loop is where most windows are actually observed and a loop passing the maximum to spend fewer calls observes a window as much as ten minutes late. The window's figure stays finishing-work's and is not copied here, since no test pins the two files' figures to each other. Nothing in the kit wakes a parked session on a timer, save a parked coordinator seat's own armed reconciliation wake, which re-invokes that seat into restating that it is parked and taking no new work. What bounds the park is the leash: the armed goal stays surfaced at session start and to the doctor, and re-arming is the recovery, so a park that never wakes is visible rather than silent. Where a dispatch is what the turn ended on, evaluate the hallmark at the first re-block after the wake, before doing anything else with that dispatch. The leash allows that stop without releasing (the goal stays armed), the completion notification re-invokes the session where a dispatch is what was pending, and the run continues under the same leash. The same literal-leading-prefix mechanics as BLOCKED apply, and a WAITING whose reason is capacity is refused the same way.

**The goal template.** A plan run's completion leash is armed in one line with `/kit-goal docs/plans/<plan>.md`. The kit-goal skill owns the canonical condition and enforces it with a deterministic kit Stop hook; the condition is met when (a) every section is complete and closed out, or (b) you are BLOCKED on a decision only I can make and have documented what you need from me and how I can provide it.

Arming costs nothing on a long run because the state outlives the session. The kit hook's state is a file in the project (`.kit/`), which no session boundary can delete, and native compaction preserves the session id, so an armed run rides its own auto-compactions with the leash intact. A session the hook cannot tie to the bound one is treated as a bystander and allowed to stop, so if a run is ever left unheld, re-arming with `/kit-goal <plan path>` is the one-line recovery and resets the binding.

**A plan arriving mid-run is itself the trigger to arm it, once its standing holds.** Whether an inbound plan carries the standing to be armed at all is settled before any arm rather than after it, and the peer-sessions skill owns that gate: the standing of the message itself, and the trace of a `## Dispatch Authorization` grant to the operator, which is mandatory and which no tool performs for you. A plan whose standing does not establish is held rather than armed. Where it does hold, receiving a new plan while a run is under way, by peer message or any other route, re-arms the queue at the earliest boundary this run's tree allows, through the kit-goal CLI the kit-goal skill spells out, in whichever of its two forms the project's state calls for. That state has three readings and not two. Where a queue is already armed, `arm --append --self-armed <plan path>` extends it under the existing binding, the flag recording that this run made the arming invocation rather than the operator. Where nothing at all is at `.kit/goal-state.json`, the append refuses, says so, and names the bare form, and the bare `arm` is what that state calls for, a first arming rather than an extension. Where something is at that path the CLI cannot read as a goal state, the append refuses too and deliberately names no command, because the bare form is a replacement: run there, it would overwrite a live leash, its queue, its history and its binding, with nothing left able to say what went. So key the bare form on the goal state being genuinely absent, which is what its own refusal reason states, rather than on the append having refused. The bare form replaces the queue rather than adding to it, so it names every plan that is to be held, the one this run is already working first and the inbound one after it: `arm --self-armed <plan in flight> <inbound plan>`. Naming the inbound plan alone would leash it and leave the run's own in-flight work unheld, invisible to the position walk, to the status line and to a blocked event's plan attribution, with no dropped-plan warning to say so, since nothing was armed to drop. Run it from the run's own cwd, since the goal family resolves from cwd and a call from the other side of a checkout boundary reads that tree's own state, finding a different leash or none at all. A tree cut before the plan's commit cannot see that path and the arm refuses, which is the drift surfacing rather than an error to route around; there the trigger fires at the next safe tree advance instead. The sender is told which of the three states the plan reached, since the handoff stays open on its side until a reply arrives: the armed acknowledgment when the arm lands, `received-authorized-deferred` naming the gate it waits on, or `received-verified-holding-for-authority` where the standing did not establish. A reply answers the handoff and ends the sender's re-sending; only the armed acknowledgment converts it. The reason is a mismatch of durability. The queue is project-scoped state on disk, which outlives any session, while the handoff message lives only in this session's context. A chapter boundary is where the kit deliberately lands its compactions, so it is exactly where an intention held in context alone dies, and once it is gone nothing shows it was ever there. That is why a plan held rather than armed goes to the Chapter in the same breath, naming what the hold waits on: arming is what makes an armed plan durable, and a held one has no record at all unless the Chapter carries it. This is a rule about when to arm and never about when to stop: a plan arriving mid-run is not in the blocker set above and is not a reason to end the turn, so the arm happens and the run continues into its next section with a longer queue behind it.

**Handoff.** When execution begins inside a conversation that was just brainstorming, say so in one line ("Spec approved, switching to autonomous execution of all N sections") so I see the mode change and can scope it down ("just §1") if I want. I should never have to set an external goal to get full execution.

## Before starting (or resuming)

Read the plan doc in full, **including all Chapters**. The Chapters are the state: they record what is done, what surprised us, and the commit model in effect. After a compaction, this re-read is mandatory before touching any file, and so is re-loading this skill: a compaction re-injects the doctrine and the SessionStart recovery block and drops everything a tool call had loaded, the plan doc's contents and this skill's body included, so if the copy of this skill you are reading arrived through a compaction summary rather than a Skill-tool invocation, it can hold the top of the section loop and not its bottom, with nothing to show where the cut fell. Re-invoke `executing-work` through the Skill tool before the next step, re-read the plan doc from disk, and re-load through ToolSearch any deferred tool schema the work ahead needs. Where the harness does leave a visible truncation notice inside a loaded skill or a tool result, that notice is the same trigger. A run that skips this executes the half it can see: the checkpoint clear at step 0 and never the open at step 8, the dispatch and never the reviewer tier bump.

**On an armed run, never stop to ask whether the plan was approved: the kit-goal skill's arming-is-approval rule already answered it.** Where the plan's `Status:` header reads anything other than `In Progress` or `Complete`, set it to `In Progress` as part of starting; a header already reading `In Progress` is left exactly as it is. `Complete` is the one value the normalization does not touch, because flipping it would be the run silently overwriting the record of a close-out rather than repairing a header: a plan a run is starting whose header says the work is finished is a contradiction between the doc and the fact of the run, and it takes the contradiction path in the blocker set below rather than a quiet flip. The kit's tooling reads three values: `Ready` for a plan authored and parked, `In Progress` for one a run has started, and `Complete` for one closed out. `Ready` is included in the normalization rather than exempt from it: it is the recognized parked value and a starting run is what moves it on, so a parked plan is listed at session start as parked rather than offered for resume, and stops being listed that way the moment a run picks it up. A value outside those three (`Draft` is the common invention) is the one that hides the plan from the SessionStart recovery inventory entirely, which is why the normalization happens at the top of the run rather than at its close. The header sits inside the approval-scoped fingerprint region the `## Assumptions` rule below describes, so where the value did change, the first Chapter this run appends records the normalization, naming what the header read and the `In Progress` it now reads, which is what marks it deliberate rather than drift; where nothing changed there is nothing to record. A worker under an external engine (the stand-down below) leaves the header to its engine.

**Then run the intake gap check on the plan doc.** List what each section does not state that its implementer would need. The listing runs here; the routing runs after the memory recall below, since memory is one of the sources a gap routes to, and every gap is routed before its section is dispatched. On an armed run, a material gap (the spec-does-not-cover decision the blocker set already names) is the `BLOCKED:` the leash already handles, named there because the `goal-blocked` event is the surface that reaches me while I am away; on an attended run it is a decision ask instead. A non-material gap, answerable from an existing source (route (a): doctrine, memory, the plan doc, the code's conventions; cite it) or covered by a low-blast reversible default (route (b): pick it and declare it), is recorded on that section's Chapter `Assumptions:` line, in brainstorming's declared-assumption line format dated today with `, section N` in the parenthetical, and the dispatch brief carries the resolved answer rather than the gap. It goes to the Chapter and never to the plan doc's `## Assumptions` section, which sits above `## Chapters` and so inside the approval-scoped fingerprint the external engine reads: a mid-run append there reads as approval drift. That section freezes at approval and holds the design-time entries alone.

**Then run `memq recall`, once, before the first section.** It returns the whole memory store as one bounded digest; the memory-system skill owns what the digest contains and how to act on it. The plan doc carries the effort's own state, and the digest carries what earlier efforts learned the hard way, which a plan written days ago cannot cite because nobody had recorded it yet. Read it against the sections ahead and carry forward what bears on them. On a resume, run it again rather than trusting a recollection of an earlier pass, and a boundary taking the memory-system skill's hand walk runs it again too, since that walk enumerates from the digest. When a recalled record changes what you build, stamp it in that turn with `memq touch <name> --applied`.

Run it from the project root, a worktree of it, or any directory inside the project. `memq` resolves the store from the session before the shell: unless an external engine pinned it, a working directory inside the project the harness filed this session under resolves that project's store, subdirectories included, and a git worktree resolves its main checkout's store (memq follows the worktree's `.git` pointer through a two-way handshake), from the worktree's own subdirectories too in a session the harness filed under the worktree, which is where it files a session started there. What resolves a different, empty store is a working directory outside the filed project, a nested independent checkout inside it (the resolver's climb stops at the nearest enclosing repository root, so a repository boundary is a store boundary), a subdirectory of a linked worktree in a session filed under the main checkout rather than the worktree (the worktree's own boundary is the ceiling there, and the plain path derivation stands), a subdirectory in a session no transcript answers for, a bare-repo worktree, or a worktree whose handshake fails (which announces itself with a `git worktree repair` note on stderr); the tell is a digest whose coverage lines all read zero on a project you know has memories.

**External-engine stand-down.** When the driving directive states that an external engine owns continuation by spawning a fresh worker per section (Spine's Dispatch pump is one), or the environment carries `KIT_EXTERNAL_ENGINE` (the marker such an engine sets at spawn), the engine is the supervisor and this session is its worker. Run the section loop for the directed section only. The marker is a directive to you, not a mechanism: treat it exactly as you would the driving directive's own words. The **worker runs this skill** - it orchestrates, dispatches implementers, and writes Chapters exactly as any session would; it does not absorb implementation inline just because it is headless.

**Workspace and siblings.** If concurrency put you in a worktree on a feature branch, that is your workspace; integration and any teardown happen in finishing-work. Expect sibling sessions to touch the same repo, so own a disjoint set of files, and never stage another session's uncommitted work or carry it in a commit of yours.

## Section loop

For each Section of Work, in order (sections run concurrently only where the disjoint-files rule in "Delegating to subagents" permits):

0. **Close the previous boundary.** If the run is leashed, clear any compaction checkpoint the last boundary left open, since work is about to resume and this is no longer a boundary:

   ```
   node <plugin-root>/hooks/kit-compact-checkpoint.js clear
   ```

   Resolve `<plugin-root>` the same way the Dispatch Brief template's style-skill bullet does (step 1 below carries that rule). It is a no-op when nothing is open, so it costs one call on the first section. It refuses at exit 1 over a record that is another session's boundary, and over a state it cannot read (a goal state that is present but unreadable, or something other than a regular file at the checkpoint path), naming the remedy in each case: the case a run meets is a resumption under a new session id against a goal still bound to its dead predecessor, and that resumed run re-arms the goal with its whole queue (`arm --self-armed`, the invocation being the run's own), which rebinds it, while any other session leaves the goal alone; what is not a checkpoint file is moved aside by hand, since no verb removes it. Two further exit-1 readings carry their remedy on the CLI's own line: a checkpoint path it cannot read right now under an unbound goal, retried once whatever holds it lets go, and a caller id the shell cannot resolve over a record that is somebody's boundary, which is why the verb runs from the session's own shell. The gate also expires a checkpoint on its own after a short window, so skipping this degrades to one mistimed compaction rather than a broken run, but the two guards are independent on purpose: a checkpoint that outlives its boundary is exactly how this feature turns into a silent no-op. That short window is not the only one, which is why this clear still matters: a checkpoint opened while the gate was already holding offers is honored far longer, for as long as the deferral episode behind it lives, so it will not quietly age out before the next section starts.

1. **Confirm the approach, then implement.** Before writing a section whose mechanism the spec assumed without reading the code, do a quick in-session read of the files it touches and confirm the planned approach holds, taking any file you are opening to find one mechanism in through the doctrine's rule on hunting in a large file, and reading whole any file that is itself the mechanism. Confirming a mechanism often spans call sites and error paths, so where you do outline, expect to read more than one of the ranges it names. A spec written during brainstorming can be fictional about code nobody had open yet. This is a lightweight read, not a fan-out; if the real shape differs materially, adjust and note it in the Chapter (raise it to me only if it changes design intent). Then implement per the section's model tier, which is a dispatch instruction rather than a hint: a section carrying a `Model:` tier goes to that tier's implementer, and the doctrine's standing dispatch request covers it, including where a session-prompt line makes dispatch conditional on my having requested it.

Where the section also changed code, that code takes the code pair over its own non-document files in the same round, because neither document lens reads a diff for correctness and a mixed section would otherwise ship its code with no section-time review at all. When omitting the docs/ paths empties the list on a section with no `Audience:` line (a docs-only section that is not a deliverable document: a plan doc edit, an index refresh), there is no diff for the blind lens to read: skip the blind dispatch, run the adversarial-reviewer alone, and record `blind: no code diff` on the Chapter's review line so the gap reads as a decision and not an omission. A standing property passes and may ride in any dispatch, the blind one included. A sentence that would change with the section fails: barred from the blind dispatch outright, and barred everywhere as pre-judging when it carries a rating. **Bracket every round with a tree-state capture.** Run `git status --porcelain` before you dispatch and again when the round returns, and compare the two before acting on a single finding.    **The compensation notch belongs to gate-shaped work and never to plan-following work**, which is the rule step 1 states and this is its ground. The two fail differently: an under-powered reviewer fails silently, since the finding it never raises leaves no trace and the gate reports green either way, while an under-powered implementer fails loudly into a review round that already exists and a tier ladder that already handles it.    This runs at every section boundary rather than waiting for the close-out, because the judgment is only cheap while it is fresh: a compaction or a session handoff can land between Chapters, and by the close-out of a long run nobody can say which of forty records changed a decision. The trivially-small section that skips its reviews does not skip this; the command is one line, and where the list is all the window owes, the answer is a recognition question over a list the machine already built.

   **Do not widen the window past the previous Chapter, on the theory that more is safer.** Widening pulls applied stamps into range as well as reads, so an earlier section's stamp on a record this section freshly read masks it, and the new use is never asked about. A wider window returns a shorter list, and the records it drops are exactly the ones a boundary sweep exists to catch.

7. **Apply the commit model** recorded in the spec header:
   - **Review-Only:** stage the section's changes (`git add`); never commit. Accumulate a running changed-files summary in the Chapter for the final walkthrough. `git diff --staged` is my review surface.
   - **Branch-and-PR:** commit the section's code together with its Chapter (the plan doc update from step 6) to the feature branch, cutting that branch before the first commit where the checkout sits on a trunk, so the record rides with the change into the eventual merge. A section's verified state commits to the branch at first green (the first-green commit: the moment it passes step 2 verification, before the step 3 review round is dispatched), with review fixes layered as follow-up commits: every branch commit is a durable recovery point, and the merge stays gated by review, the finishing pass, and the PR. The staging discipline is unchanged, whichever moment the commit lands at: the controller stages exactly the section's files, and implementers still neither commit nor stage. The PR happens in finishing-work. Pushing here is not merging: nothing is final until that merge. Those pushes land on a PR branch rather than on a trunk consumers install from, so neither fires the pre-push whole gate: the first-green push rests on the targeted lane step 2 ran at verification, which is the only run that has happened by then, and the close push on the lane step 4 ran over the section as the fixes left it. The whole gate this branch owes is the merge's, and finishing-work runs it there.
   - **Commit-and-Push:** commit the section and push to origin. Direct to main, the commit lands only here at close, never at first green, because main must never carry an unreviewed section state. If concurrency put you on a worktree branch, that branch takes Branch-and-PR's first-green commits and the lanes named there with them, and the merge to main and teardown happen in finishing-work, not here; the gate this bullet names belongs to the push to main and is not owed on the branch. **Where the push does land on main it is this step's own gate moment, and step 4's close gate does not stand in for it:** where main is a trunk consumers install from directly with no CI gating the merge, the push is itself the install surface, so the whole gate runs before it with the contention lane beside it, where step 4 ran the targeted lane over the section's own files and read nothing further out. Run it before the plan doc is staged with the Chapter: its counts and exit code go onto that Chapter's `Gate:` line beside the close gate's, so staging the doc first only means staging it again. Ordering it this way keeps the full-suite run out of the plan doc's own add-to-commit window: the doc is staged after the gate, so what sits between that add and this commit is the staged-list read. It closes nothing for the section's own files, whose window opened at step 4's scope-check `git add` and is where this gate runs. That one is the loop's standing open window rather than an oversight, the shape the doctrine's index-window bullet names when it says such a window is declared rather than shrunk: a whole gate is exactly the long-running step that bullet warns about spanning, so where a peer session may commit on this checkout, say so on the coordination surface before the gate starts. The plan doc is then staged with the counts on it, so the record this step commits names the gate that cleared the push. Writing that line is the one edit permitted after the gate, for the reason finishing-work's step 5 gives it: it records a run that has already happened and changes nothing that run read.

   Under every model, a `docs/backlog.md` entry written under step 4's out-of-scope route rides with the Chapter: staged with it under Review-Only, committed with it under the other two. An entry left dirty in the worktree with no record naming it is the leak this rule closes. The ordinary case is a backlog file whose only uncommitted lines are yours, since what a prior effort committed is already in git and is not foreign content. Where the file carries uncommitted content you did not author, the ride is off under every model and this step states no substitute for it: the disposition belongs to the doctrine's shared-file hold rule and the peer-overlap bullet beside it, to the Workspace and siblings check above, and, where a live sibling may hold the file, to the bilateral option the peer-sessions skill names. What is local to this step is that the section is not held with the entry: the section's files and the Chapter ride their carrier on schedule, and the held entry and the foreign lines are named on that Chapter's `Decisions / Surprises` line and again in the close-out, so what a fresh session recovers is a record rather than an unexplained dirty worktree, and what I get is notice of a file left dirty on my machine.

8. **Open the compaction checkpoint.** Once the Chapter is appended and the section's commit model has been honored, this is a chapter boundary, where a context compaction costs nothing. Tell the gate so:

   ```
   node <plugin-root>/hooks/kit-compact-checkpoint.js open
   ```

   Resolve `<plugin-root>` the same way the Dispatch Brief template's style-skill bullet does. The command is a no-op refusal when no kit goal is armed, so running it on an unleashed session is harmless. It refuses at exit 1 to a caller the checkpoint would not bless, the case a run meets being the same resumption under a new session id that step 0 names, with the same re-arm remedy and the same bound (the resumed run re-arms with `--self-armed`; any other session leaves the goal alone); and it writes the calling session's id from its own environment, so it runs from the session's shell rather than an operator's. It opens exactly one compaction: the gate consumes the checkpoint when the compaction lands, so the next mid-chapter attempt is deferred again. A checkpoint the gate never spends expires on its own shortly after, and step 0 of the next section clears it where it is this session's own or nobody's boundary, so an open checkpoint cannot leak into the next chapter and admit a compaction mid-section. One case is deliberately exempt from that short expiry: when the gate is already holding auto-compaction offers, the checkpoint you open here is honored for as long as the deferral episode behind it lives, rather than ageing out at ten minutes while a dispatched implementer or a whole-repo suite is still running. That episode's own life is the real bound, and it ends four hours after the gate's newest denial, so a call outrunning four hours drops back to the ten-minute leg. You do not have to do anything to get the long leg; the CLI reads the gate's state at the open and says which kind it opened.

Compaction is the harness's own: native auto-compaction may fire mid-run on a long session, and the plan doc plus its Chapters are the recovery spine. Context pressure never stops or pauses a run, and is never a reason to end a turn.

**On a gated run, compaction lands at chapter and interim boundaries by design.** With a kit goal armed and this session holding the leash, the kit's PreCompact gate defers auto-compaction mid-chapter and stands aside once step 8 or the interim ritual below has opened a checkpoint, so the compaction falls where nothing is half-finished. A deferral you notice mid-section is the gate working, never a fault and never something to act on: do not clear the goal, do not touch the checkpoint, and do not treat it as context pressure (which is never a stop condition anyway). A safety valve allows the compaction regardless once consumption nears the model's limit, so sustained deferral cannot wedge a run on the roughly 1,000,000-token window its ceiling assumes. That assumption is the one part of this that does not fail safe: on a model with a smaller window the valve would sit above the hard limit and never fire, and nothing detects that. So the standing instruction to ignore a deferral holds on a full-window model, and a run that is actually climbing toward its limit with compaction still being deferred is the one case worth surfacing to me rather than ignoring.

**A closure drought earns an interim boundary.** Two consecutive review-round adjudications with no section closing is the floor that names a drought, and a run that can see one forming may write the entry early; the count is the floor, not a gate on judgment. The count is also not the only trigger, because it depends on your noticing it: the compaction gate has been counting the same drought from the other side, and `compact-deferral-nudge.js` says so at the return of a long tool call, naming how many offers it is holding and for how long. So the count is the floor and the deferral nudge is the signal, and either one earns the entry. Treat the nudge as the more reliable of the two, since it fires from a record rather than from a recollection of how many rounds have passed. At either trigger, append an interim board entry to the plan doc below `## Chapters`, honor the commit model for the doc, then open the checkpoint with the same CLI call step 8 names; the checkpoint's own expiry is what bounds an interim boundary no compaction consumes, since step 0's clear runs only when a section starts. The entry supplements step 8 and never replaces it: it is not a Chapter and carries no `Completed:` line, and its heading is `### Interim board N - YYYY-MM-DD`, N counting the plan's interim entries the way Chapters count theirs, so two droughts in one day stay distinct; the Chapter-heading row of curating-docs' machine contract never matches it, since that row parses only the literal word `Chapter` plus a number. The entry carries, in order: each in-flight section's stage, the live dispatches and what each was asked, the current gate baseline carrying the moment the moment-pin bullet of `skills/testing-discipline/SKILL.md` under the kit plugin root requires, the rulings adopted since the last boundary, and the next action per section. Under Review-Only the entry is written and staged like everything else and the checkpoint still opens: the recovery a boundary protects is the post-compaction plan-doc re-read, which reads the worktree, so the ritual loses nothing to that model, and crash durability stays Review-Only's own standing trade-off, out of this rule's reach.

Then continue to the next section. Do not stop here.

## The consult

The consult is a single read-only fresh-context agent, the `consultant`, convened to rule on a question the session could not settle. The consult skill (`consult/SKILL.md`) owns its triggers and its mechanics: when to convene it, what the brief carries, the model rule, and how a ruling is adjudicated. Its value is that it did not see the session's transcript: the problem reaches it as text rather than as its own reasoning, so it can test the framing where a shared-context instrument can only extend it. The kit runs with no in-context counsel of any kind - no companion model shares the session's context to answer at decision points - so a second opinion arrives through fresh eyes, or through a warm peer holding context this session lacks: the consult for a question whose framing may be wrong, the expert ask for an answer that may already exist, the reviewers for a diff.

## Delegating to subagents

Tokens absorbed into the main context are re-billed on every later turn; a subagent's churn (file reads, build output, failed attempts) is paid once, and only its report comes back. The orchestrator stays the designer: it writes dispatch prompts, judges findings, reads implementer diffs, and writes Chapters. Keep a task in the main session only when it is design-entangled (its shape is still being discovered in contact with the code), tiny (the prompt would cost more than the work), or session-state-dependent (an in-flight debugging chain).

A subagent loads the skill catalog (one-line descriptions) and, where the machine's CLAUDE.md carries the kit doctrine import, the global doctrine; it loads neither the skill bodies, the conversation, your in-flight directives, nor the kit's memory context: the SessionStart memory blocks never reach a subagent, the harness injects its auto-memory index only where that feature is enabled, and the recognition nudge's dispatch boundary hands it at most a pointer, where an `agent:` trigger names its type, so assume no memory arrived and carry any memory that bears on the section in the brief. Forward every standing directive verbatim (the style contract, the exact constraint). **Write the dispatch prompt from the actual current code,** assuming a skilled engineer with zero context for this codebase: exact file paths, the signatures and types they will touch, the absolute path to each style skill it must follow, what done looks like, whether the change earns a durable test, what NOT to touch, and how to verify. **Hand bulky inputs over as files,** not pasted inline: the spec, or a diff captured with `git diff > .kit/scratch/<name>.diff` (gitignored, never `docs/`; keep the project's `.gitignore` covering `.kit/`). The blind-reviewer is the standing exception to the diff-file handoff: it gets the base ref or changed-file list per step 3's contract, never a captured diff, because the capture's filename (usually the section name) and any docs/ hunks inside it are the intent story arriving through a side door. **A subagent's report comes back in its final message, not as a committed file.** The implementer's status protocol and the reviewers' findings lists are terse by design, so have each return its report inline and distill the durable outcome into the Chapter. A large review can still use the summary-plus-file pattern: return the verdict, the Critical/Major/Minor counts, and the top finding inline, and write the full findings to a file the orchestrator reads only when adjudicating. The tell that you are repeating the old failure is a dispatch line like `write your full findings to docs/reviews/...` or `docs/plans/_impl_reports/...`: a report is a transient working artifact and the Chapter is the curated record, so route those to `.kit/` or take them inline. The same discipline applies to read-only scouts, whose return contract is below. Parallelize only when tasks touch non-overlapping files; lock shared contracts first and assign disjoint files.

**Stagger concurrent sections; lockstep is the anti-pattern.** When several sections run at once, advance them offset (one being briefed, one implementing, one in review), and run steps 4 through 8 for a section the moment it individually clears adjudication, never batched with siblings (adjudication is step 4 itself, so the trigger reads: the moment a round's step 4 ends with nothing blocking the section's close, a fixes-then-re-review cycle clearing at its final round's adjudication, steps 5 through 8 follow immediately for that section). Starting sections together and marching them through review rounds together is the one arrangement that yields zero boundaries at identical parallelism and identical wall-clock: no Chapter closes, no commit lands, and the compaction gate defers every offer while the worktree holds the finished work's only copy. A step 8 close reached while sibling sections are still in flight appends an interim board entry alongside its Chapter, carrying the content list the closure-drought rule names, because a Chapter records the closed section alone and the siblings' live dispatches, briefs, and adopted rulings otherwise exist only in context.

**A brief grants nothing a mechanical guard denies.** Where a PreToolUse guard keys on agent type, widening the agent's file scope in the brief text is inert: the write is blocked whatever the brief says, and the round is spent discovering the denial. Route around the guard at dispatch time instead: send an agent type the guard admits, keep the guarded write in the main thread, or tell the agent up front to return the text in its report for the main thread to place. The `docs/` routing override in step 1 is this rule's standing instance.

**A brief forbids nothing the guard does not govern.** The mirror of the rule above: the readonly-agent-guard binds a fixed roster, the strict reviewer class plus the qa-verifier, so a scout dispatched as Explore or general-purpose, or a Workflow `agent()` call naming no `agentType`, carries Bash the guard never reads, and its brief's read-only instruction is a request, not a control. Every read-only-intent dispatch under an ungoverned type therefore takes its own tree-state bracket: `git status --porcelain` before dispatch and again at return, any delta taking the same incident path as step 3's round bracket (restore the tree, record the delta and the agent in the Chapter, treat its report as suspect). The named types are instances, not the boundary: the governed set is the guard's own classifier, so a type you cannot place as governed gets the bracket.

**Band the scout by question shape, and state its return contract.** A closed fact-check (does X contain Y, confirm a value, verify a checklist item) rides the harness default, because the mandatory confirmation of load-bearing leads makes a wrong answer self-surfacing; open discovery (map a surface, find every call site) gets an explicit sonnet override, because the failure confirmation cannot catch is the miss, and a missed site silently narrows the design. Top-model recon is pure burn either way, and a "simple check" that comes back with more than a couple of leads was mis-banded: re-run it as discovery. Every scout dispatch states its return contract in the prompt, so the report lands lean by construction: each lead comes back as a file:line reference with a one-sentence fact and why the site matters, never pasted file contents, and bulky evidence goes to the gitignored `.kit/` scratch path, read on demand.

**Serialize what the environment cannot share.** Implementation stays single-agent-per-worktree when it touches shared state, and the long integration suites run through one controller: two concurrent runs against a single shared database collide, fail in a heap, and orphan test state. The single-shared-resource constraint dominates orchestration design more than any parallelize-by-default instinct.

## Chapter format

Append to the `## Chapters` section of the plan doc:

```markdown
### Chapter N - YYYY-MM-DD
Completed: <section name>
Implemented By: <main session | implementer-haiku | implementer-sonnet | implementer-opus | implementer-fable, plus any escalation>
Metrics: <review rounds <n>, closed <clean | claim-exit | major-closed>; NEEDS_CONTEXT count; escalations; consults <n>>
Recap: <omitted from a section Chapter, carried on the finishing Chapter alone; the plain-language recap whose parts and order finishing-work's step 5 owns>
Decisions / Surprises: <anything resolved or discovered; "none" is acceptable; in the kit's own repository, one line for the probe pair reading writing-skills' RED and GREEN step calls for, or the state that step names instead, or that no scenario turned on so no pair ran, or that the change named no shape file, none of which is recorded as clean, with its moment-pin where a pair ran>
Assumptions: <the declared-assumption entries recorded during this section; "none" is acceptable>
Review Findings: <`review: <pair or lens>[ (<n> readers)] at <model>, <route>[ (<chain>)]`, per dispatch where a pair split, or `review: <pair or lens> ungated (<chain>)`, `blind: no code diff` beside it where the blind lens was skipped; then Critical/Major addressed; Majors justified; Minors noted>
Stamps: <adjudicated N, stamped M; "none surfaced" is acceptable where the stretch was accounted for; a window that owed a hand walk says so, why, and what the walk found>
Gate: <the lane or lanes that ran: the targeted lane at every section close; the contention lane beside it where the section's delta touched machine-shared state; the whole gate where step 7's push lands on an install-surface trunk; and the contention lane again beside that whole gate, which is a second trigger rather than the same one; their counts as tests/pass/fail and the exit code read from the run itself, the code itself rather than a statement that it was read; the delta against the baseline recorded on that same lane, or that no baseline exists on it; any contention the run carried; and, in the kit's own repository and on a final Chapter alone, the probe set's reading where finishing-work's step 5 calls for one: for each invocation of each leg that step calls for, the summary line the runner prints to stdout, whose fields the runner's own README owns, quoted verbatim except that a path it printed in full, there or in a quoted refusal reason, is respelled repo-relative or left out on the Delta line's rule, with the process exit code beside it where the two differ, the warning lines of the run's report.md beside it where any printed, the mismatched moments by name with each row's status, and its moment-pin as the Delta line below carries its own; or the state that step names instead, or that the changeset named no shape file so no run was called for, none of which is recorded as clean, a before leg the after leg gave no moments for being none of them, a `(partial)` line marked as the leg its re-run replaced, and the exit code marked unreadable where the doctrine's fallback path left none; a reading that reports and never blocks>
Next: <next section, or "finishing-work">
Commit Model: <Review-Only | Branch-and-PR | Commit-and-Push>
Delta: <the moment-pin for the reading below, in the form this format's own paragraph requires of every measured figure a Chapter records, since the verb's output carries no machine and no contention of its own; then the reading `node <plugin-root>/scripts/kit-size.js report --repo <the project's root>` prints, quoted in a fenced block below this line exactly as it prints it, whatever rows, totals or non-output line it holds>
```

Chapters exist so that a compacted or fresh session can recover full working state from the plan doc alone. Write them for that reader. The Metrics line doubles as the data feed for the kit's open experiments (the tier-band question and the consult-adoption watch in `docs/backlog.md`), so record it even when every count is zero. Its `closed` is read off the last round that carried findings: `major-closed` where that round carried an owed Major, `claim-exit` where it carried a claim finding and no such Major, and `clean` otherwise, a section with no round included. The Assumptions line is where an execution-time assumption is recorded, so the close-out computes its assumptions block from the Chapters rather than re-reading the run; the plan doc's `## Assumptions` section freezes at approval and holds the design-time entries alone. The Delta line resolves `<plugin-root>` the same way the Dispatch Brief template's style-skill bullet does. It names the repository outright because the reading's default root is derived from where the script itself sits, which under a marketplace install is inside the plugin payload rather than inside the project. It quotes the verb and states no condition of its own. Which rows that output holds, which it omits, whether this project is one the reading measures at all, and what each of its three non-output lines means are the verb's own answers, stated at `scripts/kit-size.js` rather than copied here, where a copy could only go stale against the tool. One rule the field imposes on all three: any path they carry is spelled relative to the project's root or left out, on the same rule the BLOCKED first line already follows, because an absolute checkout path carries the operator's user name into a tracked document. It sits last on the template for two reasons. Its output is a fenced block the tool prints, several lines where an author-written field is one or a few; the Gate line carrying probe legs and the finishing Chapter's assumptions block are two of the author-written multi-line fields, each sitting between the contract lines, and the rule on their writer stated below holds over any such field. The Chapter heading with its `Completed:` and `Next:` lines is also a machine contract external tooling parses, so the Delta output sits after every line that contract reads rather than between them. The fence is no barrier to that parser, which reads a Chapter body line by line. What keeps the reading harmless is that no line the verb prints can open with `Completed:` or `Next:`: a row opens with a path under a measured root and every other line with a label of the verb's own. The verb also strips a leading markdown heading marker from any path it prints, which would otherwise end the parser's Chapters block. The Gate line's probe legs sit between the contract lines instead, so the rule on their writer is the whole protection, and it holds over every line an author adds to any Chapter field, the finishing Chapter's assumptions block among them: no such line, quoted or free, opens with `Completed:`, `Next:` or `#`, and a recorded text that would is respelled there with the respelling noted. The runner's summary line opens `probe-corpus:` and the report's warning lines `- WARNING:`, which is what lets both be quoted verbatim. What the reading compares is the worktree against HEAD, so what it covers depends on where HEAD stands when it is taken. Under a commit model where HEAD does not move during the plan it reports the accumulated delta of every section since. Under one whose section commit lands before the Chapter is written it covers the delta since that commit rather than the section whole. On a shared checkout it carries any other session's uncommitted edits under the measured roots too, which is why the row list is worth reading rather than only the totals. The Gate line is what makes a mid-plan red readable: under the cadence the operating doctrine's gate bullet sets, a section's green covers the lanes that ran rather than the tree, except where step 7's push to an install-surface trunk pulled a whole gate up to that close, so a reader diagnosing a red in a family no section touched cannot tell what any Chapter's green covered unless the Chapter names its lanes. A Chapter is a journal-layer artifact, so every measured figure it records carries its moment in the form the moment-pin bullet of `skills/testing-discipline/SKILL.md` under the kit plugin root states, which is what lets a later reader tell a live number from one the machine expired, and the deep evidence behind a figure keeps the home that bullet gives it, so the Chapter cites the analysis rather than restating it.

The Chapter heading and its `Completed:`/`Next:` lines are a machine contract read by external tooling, not just kit convention; see `curating-docs/SKILL.md`'s machine contract section for the frozen shape and which values register a section complete.

## When all sections are complete

Invoke the finishing-work skill. Do not declare the effort done without it. This holds under Review-Only: finishing-work still flips the plan to Complete, archives it, and stages it with the code. Review-Only defers the commit to me, never the doc's finalization, so the plan is never left open for me to close.
